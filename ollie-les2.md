# Puppy Training Course — Theory Lesson 2: Hierarchy, Communication & Heeling

## Overview

This second theory lesson of the puppy training course covers three major topics: the six hierarchy rules between owner and dog, dog-to-dog communication and body language, and the practical exercises for the week (the "follow/heel" exercise steps 1–3 and the "magic word" recall). Trainer **Chantal** leads the session with assistant trainer **Martijn**. The lesson also includes video analysis of common mistakes, Q&A with participants (including a discussion about Ollie, a Golden Retriever puppy who refuses kibble), bite incident protocols, and real-world case studies that illustrate the concepts.

Chantal emphasizes throughout: these hierarchy rules are **not black and white**. They must be adapted to your individual dog's temperament — stricter for bold/dominant dogs, gentler for soft/insecure ones.

## 1. Hierarchy Rules Between Owner and Dog

### The Philosophy: Not Black and White

Chantal addresses the controversy head-on: on the internet, you'll find claims that dominance/hierarchy rules are "outdated" and "no longer relevant." She disagrees — partially. What's outdated is applying these rules **rigidly and identically** to every dog. She has never taught them black-and-white.

Growing up with a litter of Tervuren Shepherds at age 19, she observed the mother dog being very strict (black-and-white) with one pup but very lenient (grey) with another. Initially, young Chantal thought it was because the stricter-treated pup had a white blaze — an "imperfection" in the breed. She now recognizes this as a very human (and flawed) way of thinking: the mother treated that puppy more strictly because it was **bolder and more dominant**, not because of its appearance.

Hierarchy exists everywhere in nature — in horses, gerbils, wolves, migrating birds, and yes, in dogs. Even in human society: we wait for everyone to be served before eating at a restaurant; a funeral procession takes priority on the road; a football match doesn't start until the referee says so.

**The key distinction:**
- **Bold/dominant dog:** Apply the rules more strictly. Be clear about boundaries. Once the dog understands and respects the hierarchy, you can gradually give more freedom.
- **Soft/insecure dog:** Apply the rules very gently. Sometimes you deliberately *break* the rules to build the dog's confidence and character. For example, instead of making a soft dog move out of your way, walk around it with a wide arc so it feels safe lying wherever it wants.

The wolves study is relevant here: wild wolves observed in natural settings show much more nuanced social behavior than wolves in zoos (which were artificially assembled from different regions and showed extreme black-and-white dominance). The same applies to our dogs: context matters.

### Rule 1: The Leader Walks in Front

**Core principle:** Whoever walks in front is the pack leader.

**When off-leash outdoors:**
- Walk unpredictably. When the dog runs 20 meters ahead, suddenly turn and walk in a completely different direction — off the path, across the grass, through the bushes. Wear old shoes and go on an adventure.
- The dog will be surprised, look back, run after you, pass you — and the moment it passes you, **turn your back** and walk the other direction again.
- **Never turn your face toward the dog** when it passes you, as it will interpret that as attention/engagement. Always turn your back.

**Door training:**
- Applies to all doorways: crate door, living room door, front door, terrace door, restaurant doors, doors at friends' houses.
- Open the door. The dog tries to go through → slam the door shut (even if it bumps its nose or shoulder). Say **"No"** (in English).
- Repeat: open door, dog pushes → door closes. "No."
- The goal is not making the dog sit-stay while you go through first. The goal is the dog **voluntarily** letting you go first out of respect — genuinely choosing to wait, not being commanded to wait.
- Also applies to narrow passages (e.g., a tight path with prickly bushes on both sides) — the dog must wait and let you through.

**Dominant dogs** will push their nose between your knees at doorways. **Submissive dogs** will wait a meter and a half behind you.

**Hide and Seek:**
- When off-leash, play hide and seek in nature. This is excellent for preventing the dog from constantly running away from you.
- Remember: dogs have far superior senses. Hide properly — not behind the first tree by the path. Run into the bushes, account for wind direction (so the dog can't smell you immediately).
- Let the dog experience a moment of genuine stress from losing you. This ensures it won't happen a second time.
- Even on the beach: lie flat on your belly, head down. The dog searches for a silhouette of a standing person with two legs and a torso — you're already "hidden." Behind a trash can where your head sticks out above it? Still hidden, because the dog is looking for a moving figure.

### Rule 2: The Dog Must Make Way for You

**In the home:**
- When the dog lies in prominent spots in walkways (kitchen entrance, living room doorway), shuffle toward it with your feet while saying "No" (English).
- The dog learns to respect your personal space and move aside.
- **For dominant dogs:** They deliberately lie in high-traffic spots to "score points" — they want to be noticed, stepped over, walked around. Make them move.
- **For soft/insecure dogs:** Walk around them with a wide arc. These dogs choose hiding spots because they're afraid of being seen. You *want* them to feel safe lying anywhere.

**Outside:**
- If the dog walks in front of your feet, march with your legs stretched forward. If it gets bumped, that's its problem. "I just happened to feel like stretching my legs."
- This prevents dangerous situations: dogs darting between your feet can cause falls — not a big deal when you're young, but potentially hip-breaking when you're older.
- The more you assert your physical presence, the more the dog learns to wait and assess safety before crossing paths, roads, or bike lanes — the same awareness it needs around joggers, cyclists, and other people in public.
- Same principle with a bicycle: if the dog runs in front of the bike, tap it with the handlebars. Better a controlled learning moment from you than a collision with a stranger's bike.

### Rule 3: You Start and End Play

**Toy management:**
- Pick up all toys from the floor. You are the one who initiates play.
- Leave *one* toy on the ground. If the dog plays with it on its own — fine, ignore it. When *you* want to play, get a *different* toy from the cupboard and deliberately start the game.

**Teaching "Drop It" (Los):**
- Practice give-and-take: hold a treat against the dog's nose → it releases the toy → treat. This teaches that "drop it" means "you get a good trade."
- Build up to the dog understanding that releasing an object is always rewarded.

**When the dog grabs aggressively:**
- If the dog lunges uncontrollably at your hands while you're picking up a toy — give a clear "No" (English) and an appropriate correction.
- Match the correction to the dog's sensitivity: for some dogs, a firm "No" with looming body language and a growl-like sound is enough. For very bold dogs, a tap may be needed.
- Think about it from the dog's perspective: it would never grab uncontrollably from its mother, father, or siblings — they'd immediately correct it. You deserve the same respect.

**Controlling chase/hunt/swim games:**
- Chasing (frisbee, ball): Only when *you* say so. Dogs screaming from the sideline at a frisbee competition are burning energy they should save. You didn't say "go" — so you wait.
- The hunting instinct: Sheep chasing, bird chasing, chasing other dogs' hunting groups — the dog only chases what you allow it to chase.
- Swimming: The dog doesn't get to decide when it goes in the water. Use a triple command: "Swim, swim, swim!" (triple so a single accidental utterance doesn't trigger it — dogs don't understand negation, so "don't swim" doesn't work; they just hear "swim").
- You can integrate the water as a reward for good heeling: heel well → click → permission to swim. Come back, heel again → click → swim again. The water becomes the reward.

**The case study of the sheep-chasing dog** (discussed during the introduction): A client's second dog, properly trained, was left with family during a skiing holiday. Despite instructions to keep the dog on-leash, they let it run free near a ditch with sheep. Day one: running back and forth, a ditch between them — looked funny. Day two: the dog jumped the ditch, chased two sheep into the water, and severely injured one. Months of work followed at the behavioral consultation to rehabilitate the dog around farm animals, ducks, geese — anything moving on the ground. This is why the farm animal workshop exists and is **necessary**, not just fun.

### Rule 4: The Dog Only Approaches When Asked

**Uninvited contact:**
- If the dog constantly nudges under your arm, pushes its nose at you demanding attention — it's deciding when contact happens. For a soft dog, this is relatively harmless, but for a dominant dog it's a problem.
- A dog that gets unlimited affection all day — from you, three kids, your partner, the au pair, the neighbor who can't keep her hands off — why would it find your attention special enough to come back for it outside?
- Service/assistance dogs that are cuddled too much lose their work drive — the same principle applies.

**Be deliberate with affection:**
- Make cuddles special, not constant. Not just when you feel like it, but consciously — and not every time the dog seeks it.
- This directly connects to recall: if the dog has you "in its pocket" at home, why would it bother coming to you when there are interesting smells and other dogs outside?

**Children and visitors:**
- Teach children: "May I ask your dog if it wants to be petted?" — not just asking the owner, but then crouching low, hands low, letting the dog choose to approach.
- Never reach over a dog's head — they don't actually enjoy that.

**The hotel incident:** Chantal was at a family hotel in Zeeland with her two trained dogs (11+ years old). She left her older female under a six-person table while helping her 3-year-old son at the buffet. She returned to find a large man crouched under the table, pulling his hand back — the dog had snapped at him. The dog had never done anything like that in her life, but she felt unsafe and unprotected. Chantal was upset not at the dog but at herself for leaving her dog vulnerable, and at the man for approaching without permission. She told the entire dining room: "You should understand that you don't approach a dog without asking. My dog was assaulted while I, her leader, wasn't there to protect her." The takeaway: even perfectly trained dogs will react when they feel threatened and their leader isn't present to protect them.

### Rule 5: Higher Position = Pack Leader

**Ground-level behavior:**
- When you sit on the ground with a bold/dominant dog, expect more mouthy/nippy/wild behavior — you've lowered yourself.
- When you sit on the ground with a soft/insecure dog, it may roll onto its back next to you — this is submission, not a request for belly rubs.

**The belly exposure myth:**
- A dog rolling onto its back is showing submission — giving itself over to you. It does **not** mean "please rub my belly."
- When a dog does this for another dog, the other dog briefly sniffs, maybe hovers above — and walks away. That's all. But humans see an exposed belly and start vigorously petting. The dog didn't ask for that.
- Some young puppies may even urinate when in this position — it's pure submission.
- **Exception:** If a dog is already lying on its side, relaxed, and you sit next to it and scratch it, and it starts doing the "scratching along" motion with its leg — then you've found a spot it genuinely enjoys. But that's a *relaxed on its side* position, not a *rolled onto its back in submission* position.

**Couch rules:**
- A dog is allowed on the couch — *if* you don't have rank issues and the dog isn't one that can't be alone.
- The dog comes up when *you* say so, and gets off when *you* say so.
- Chantal's male dog would beg to get on the couch, she'd send him away, then after five minutes invite him up. Sometimes he'd then want to leave — and she'd make him stay: "We're not married like that, buddy — now you stay until *I* say you can go." (With unfamiliar dogs: if they've had enough contact, let them walk away — that's fine.)

**Jumping up:**
- A dog jumping up can mean different things: submission (trying to lick your mouth/hands), fear ("pick me up, it's scary down here"), or dominant pushiness (forcing contact you didn't initiate).
- For dominant jumping: bring your knee up (large dog → hits your knee; small dog → hits your foot). Say "No" (English).
- In natural dog packs: a subordinate dog approaches a returning high-ranking dog by taking 1.5 meters distance, then crawling low to ask permission to enter the other's space. Jumping with two paws into the neck of a higher-ranking dog gets an immediate, harsh correction.

### Rule 6: You Eat First, Then the Dog

**Food bowl training:**
- Lower the bowl. The instant the dog dives for it → pull it back up. "No."
- Wait about 20 seconds. Lower again. Dog dives → pull up. "No."
- **No "sit" command, no "wait" command.** Same principle as the door: you don't command — the dog learns to voluntarily wait, look at you, and only eat when you say "Enjoy your meal" or "Go ahead."
- Once the dog is eating, leave it alone.

**Teaching gentle mouth/soft takes:**
- Dog grabs at treats aggressively, biting your whole hand? Close your fist around the treat, push it gently against the dog's mouth. "No." "You wanted a treat? Here it is — in a fist. If you want the treat, take *the treat*, not the whole fist."
- The dog learns to use its lips and tongue carefully, picking the treat gently from your hand.
- Chantal as a teenager used to hold a breadstick in her mouth and let young puppies learn to take it gently — her nose and cheeks stayed intact. An unconventional but effective natural hierarchy exercise.

### The Correction Word: "No" (English)

The correction word is **"No"** spoken in English, because:
- The Dutch "nee" is used constantly in daily conversation ("Is it in that cupboard?" "No, it's in the other room") — the dog would think it's being corrected when it's not.
- English "No" is distinct and can be reserved exclusively for the dog.

**Exception:** If your dog's name contains the "no" sound — Dino, Bono, Nomi, Noah, Bruno, Bo, Kato — then "No" won't work. Choose a different neutral correction word (but not the Dutch "nee").

## 2. Dog-to-Dog Communication & Body Language

### Tail Position & What It Means

- **High and wagging broadly** (almost touching the ground on each side): Confident, self-assured dog. Anal glands wide open — broadcasting its scent. "I'm here, smell me!" Very bold dogs (especially young males around 10 months) can provoke older dogs this way, like a teenager who needs to learn to use deodorant.
- **Low and fast wagging**: Insecure tail wagging. The dog is tense, not at ease. Chantal compares it to "laughing like a farmer with toothache" (*lachen als een boer met kiespijn*) — her mother used to laugh exaggeratedly at her dominant father's inappropriate jokes, trying to defuse the situation while not really finding it funny. That nervous, forced social response is what an insecure tail wag looks like.
- **Ears also matter**: Ears down with insecure tail = the dog doesn't know what to feel. Ears forward = engagement/confidence.

### Ambivalent Behavior

Dogs can show **contradictory signals** simultaneously:
- Ears forward but tail dropping during barking = the dog wants to attack but doesn't dare. The bark is bravado.
- Tail straight up but ears back = mixed signals.

Learning to read these combinations is important. When your dog barks at another dog, observe: is *everything* forward (ears, tail, body) — meaning genuine aggressive intent? Or is part of the body showing insecurity — meaning the dog is bluffing, unsure, "doing something and hoping it works out"?

### On-Leash Communication Problems

This is why dogs who are fine off-leash become reactive on-leash:

When two leashed dogs approach each other, Dog A says: "Halt. Stand still. Show me your signals." Dog B starts slowing down, but its owner walks on, dragging it forward. Dog A repeats: "HALT. Stand still!" Dog B is being pulled closer. Dog A escalates: fixating → growling → barking. Dog B tries to move to the other side of its owner for distance, but the owner pulls it back to the left side.

**We break their communication** by not listening to their body language.

**What you should do:**
- If your dog stops and stares at another dog — **you stop too**. Your dog sees something it needs to process.
- If the approaching dog is staring but trying to slow down (and its owner is pulling it forward), recognize: that dog is trying to communicate properly but can't because of its owner.
- Don't say "Come, walk on, it's nothing." Stand still and let your dog complete its assessment.

Chantal's own practice: she steps off the path with her two Australian Shepherds, giving the other dog space. The other dog stops, looks away (asking permission to pass), and her dogs wait. The other owner says "Come on, don't be silly" and drags the dog past. The dog wasn't being silly — it was communicating perfectly. The owner just didn't see it.

### Corrections by Other Dogs — Let It Happen!

Chantal says explicitly: **"I hope your puppies get bitten soon."**

She means: puppies need to be corrected by other dogs. They need to squeal like piglets. They need to sit on their blisters for a moment.

**After a correction:**
- Don't touch, comfort, or examine the puppy immediately. Let it process what happened.
- Don't pick it up or coddle it — that rewards the insecurity and creates a trauma blueprint.
- Ideally, ask the other owner to stay for a moment so your puppy can see the other dog is not dangerous — just sniffing around, doing its thing.
- Walk together for a bit afterward. Your puppy will keep 3 meters of distance — and that's fantastic. It's learning far more from that distance than from the initial correction alone.

**Mother dogs matter:** Puppies from mothers who stayed with the litter longer received more communication and correction. If a breeder separated the mother at 5–6 weeks because she was "being mean" to the pups, those puppies missed crucial socialization from mom.

### Approaching Dogs Politely (for humans)

- Always ask the owner: "May I ask your dog if it wants to be petted?"
- Then crouch low, hands low, passive posture — let the dog come to you.
- Never reach over the head.
- The dog chooses. If it comes to you — fine. If not — respect that.

**Don't interfere with communication:** If your dog is fixating on another dog, don't shove a treat in its face to distract it — the other dog will misread the averted gaze as permission to approach, then your dog whips around furious: "I already told you to back off! Weren't you watching?"

### Social Behavior Explained

Chantal asked the class: "What is socially aware behavior?" Answers included: observing, looking away, knowing how to relate to other dogs, playing with consideration, not approaching insecure/fearful dogs, allowing butt-sniffing.

Her addition: even before the butt-sniffing, there needs to be **permission**. A fixating dog must first stand still, look left, look right — essentially asking "May I approach?" The other dog mirrors this: looking away, sniffing the ground — "Yes, you may." Only then does the approach happen.

**Leash etiquette:** If you see a dog on a leash — leash your dog too, even in an off-leash area. You don't know why that dog is on a leash (recovering from surgery, doesn't recall well, isn't the owner's dog, is reactive). This simple act prevents so much conflict. Chantal's own dogs stay within 5–10 meters of her and don't approach leashed dogs because it's "not important."

**Third-party dynamics:** Two dogs playing happily together doesn't mean a third dog is welcome. Just like at a café: "Is this chair taken?" "Yes." The two friends don't need a third party.

## 3. The "Follow" Exercise (Heeling — Steps 1–3)

### Setup

- **Clicker** and the end of the leash in your **left hand**
- **Treats** in your **right hand**
- The dog stands **in front of you**
- Hold the treat hand **on the dog's nose**
- Keep the hand about **30 cm away from your knee** (so the dog walks beside you, not pressed against your leg)

### Step 1: Walking Backwards

- Walk backward with your hand on the dog's nose, guiding it forward toward you
- After a few steps, **click**
- **Stand completely still** after clicking
- Give the treat
- **Wait** until the dog finishes eating
- Then get its attention and restart

**Key points:**
- Take at least 2–3 steps before clicking (don't click after just one touch-and-go)
- Keep the hand low enough that the dog doesn't need to jump — even if that means very low for a small puppy
- The hand stays connected to the dog's nose — don't lift it away to dig in your pocket for another treat. Keep the hand loaded.

### Step 2: Arm Extended Sideways

- Start in Step 1 (two steps backward, hand on nose)
- Then stand upright and extend your right arm **straight out to the side, at face/eye height**
- **Not at hip height** — this is the most common mistake. The arm must be fully extended and high enough that it's in line with your face, so the dog looks UP toward your head.
- Click when the dog follows and looks up at your hand
- Give treat, stand still, wait for the dog to finish eating
- **Always restart from Step 1** — a few steps backward, then pull up to Step 2. You don't always need to treat in Step 1; sometimes you flow straight through.

If the dog doesn't look up: you moved your hand up too fast — go slower. If the dog jumps at your hand: your arm isn't horizontal enough; it's too low.

### Step 3: The Rotation (Pivot)

The dog is on your right side (following your extended right arm). It needs to end up on your **left** side (standard heeling position).

**The technique:**
- While your arm is extended to the right and the dog is walking under it…
- Rotate your **left shoulder into your arm** — your body turns so you're now facing forward, but your arm stays in the same position in space
- The dog's position doesn't change — it's still under the same arm in the same spot — but now it's on your left side
- Click when the dog walks beside you, treat, stop

**Practice tip:** Put your hand against a wall. The hand cannot move. Now rotate your body. That's the feeling — the hand stays fixed, your body turns around it.

**Dry practice:** Everyone should extend their right arm and practice the rotation at home, without the dog, to build muscle memory.

### Training the "Free" Release Word

When the dog doesn't need to heel, it needs to know that too. Train "**Free**" (*Vrij*):

- Throw a few leaves in the air → dog chases them → say "Free!"
- Toss 3 treats in the grass → say "Search, search, free!"
- During free walking, keep repeating "Free, free, free" so the dog associates the word with being released from the exercise.

### Common Mistakes (Video Analysis)

A participant sent Chantal two videos — one inside (going well) and one outside (going terribly).

**Indoor video — minor issues:**
- No leash (should always practice with leash)
- Otherwise good: correct hand position, clicker in correct hand, treats in correct hand, standing still during treats

**Outdoor video — multiple errors identified by the class:**
1. **Walking forward** instead of backward (Step 1 requires backward walking)
2. **Using the wrong hand** (left instead of right)
3. **No click**, only treats
4. **Not standing still** during treat delivery — the dog is still chewing while she walks on, loses focus, gets distracted by leaves
5. **Arm too low** in Step 2 (hip height instead of face height) — loses the dog in turns
6. **Too few steps** in Step 1 before transitioning
7. **Using the dog's name excessively** — "Teun, Teun, Teun" — without rewarding when the dog actually responded to its name
8. **Not rewarding** good responses — the dog checked in multiple times and got nothing

The participant was shocked when Chantal pointed out how often she said the name without rewarding, and how rarely she rewarded the good behavior. She completely changed her approach and the outdoor heeling improved dramatically.

**Advice:** Film each other training. Watch yourself. You'll immediately spot: "Oh, I'm not standing still. My hand is too low. I said his name five times without rewarding."

### Location Progression

All exercises follow this progression:
1. **Living room** — 5-minute sessions with breaks (do something else between sessions: load the dishwasher, make a phone call)
2. **Just outside your front door** — once indoor training is solid, STOP training indoors and move outside
3. **Further down the street** — once the doorstep is solid
4. **Different locations** — vary as much as possible
5. **High-distraction environments** — the beach, the city, train stations

Chantal showed videos of advanced students heeling on the beach (with all its distractions), near a central train station, and on city streets. The goal: the dog heels reliably everywhere.

**Why this matters on the beach specifically:** On sunny days, the beach is flooded with dogs that can't be off-leash anywhere else — their owners go to the beach thinking it's "safe." Rainy days reveal the well-trained regulars. Keep your dog under control there too.

## 4. The Magic Word (Reliable Recall)

### Choosing the Right Word

The "magic word" (*toverwoord*) replaces the dog's name for recall, because owners use the name far too often without meaning or reward — neutralizing it completely.

**Requirements:**
- A word you can ONLY say happily/positively — impossible to say angrily
- Not used in daily conversation
- Not the dog's name
- Not too long
- Something you're willing to shout in public (on the beach, in the park)
- Words ending in "ie/y" work well because of the cheerful sound
- Examples: "Happy," "Cookie," "Spookie," "Chickie," "Feestie" (party)

**What NOT to choose:**
- Pet names you already use ("sweetie," "buddy") — too common
- Embarrassing words you won't actually use — one client chose "Tinky Winky" (from Teletubbies). Her husband had to yell it in the forest. Everyone died laughing, but it was genuinely hard for him.
- Words that can be said negatively

### Magic Treats (what to use)

This is NOT the time for regular kibble. Use **special, high-value treats** the dog doesn't normally get:

- Boiled chicken breast (cut small)
- Young cheese (gouda — low salt, perfectly healthy)
- Dried sprats (small dried fish — dogs love them)
- Other genuinely special treats

**Chantal is emphatic:** "Nobody is telling me Thursday that they haven't trained this or haven't used special treats. And nobody is saying 'but that's human food' or 'he'll start drooling.' Nonsense. Boiled chicken and young cheese are healthy. There's barely any salt in young cheese. This is the easiest, most important, and most fun exercise I'll give you. Go all out."

### Training Steps

1. **Crouch down**, say the magic word enthusiastically, reward when the dog comes
2. Once the dog comes reliably: add a **sit** before rewarding (first a treat for coming, then a treat for sitting)
3. Once that works: **stand up** while calling
4. Add **distractions**: place a snuffle mat on the ground → dog is sniffing → call the magic word → click when the dog turns toward you → reward immediately

The progression mirrors all other exercises: easy → harder → harder → real-world.

### The Hand Signal

The magic word also goes on a **hand signal**: both arms waving/moving. This is critical because:
- On a crowded beach, among dozens of people, the dog can visually identify *you* by the hand signal
- When dogs age and become hard of hearing (like Chantal's current 13-year-old), the hand signal still works perfectly

**If you don't have magic treats on you: you may NOT use the magic word.** During the learning phase, every use must be backed by the special reward. Never dilute it.

## 5. Bite Incidents: The Wheelbarrow Method

### When to Intervene

A true bite emergency is when a dog **shakes** another dog in its mouth — not a quick snap or correction, but sustained, violent shaking (treating the other dog as prey).

### The Wheelbarrow Method

- Grab the attacking dog's **hind legs** and lift them up (like a wheelbarrow)
- **Never** grab the dog by the neck/scruff — it can spin around and bite your hands
- **Never** pull on a hind leg — always lift both
- The dog becomes disoriented, releases its grip, and looks backward
- The other dog can escape
- You cannot be bitten in this position

### Recovery Protocol

After a serious bite incident:
1. **Don't touch** the bitten dog immediately — don't examine, comfort, or pick it up
2. Let the dog **recover on its own** — watch its tail, ears, and behavior
3. Recovery signs: tail comes back up, ears normalize, dog starts sniffing around again
4. After 8–10 minutes, **scatter treats in the grass** and see if the dog wants to eat — if yes, it's recovering
5. **Then** you may examine for wounds
6. Minor wounds: clean with lukewarm water and ointment (*olie-zalf*)
7. **Go to the vet** only if: a wound keeps bleeding without forming a scab (may need a staple — usually not stitched, because bite wounds need to drain)
8. Dogs heal many of these injuries well on their own

## 6. Q&A: Older Dog vs. New Puppy

A participant (Krijgsman family) asked: their older dog (12 years old) barks at the new puppy and shows teeth when the puppy is too energetic, but doesn't follow through with a real correction. Now the puppy is starting to bark *back*. Should they intervene?

**Chantal's analysis:**
- A 12-year-old dog likely has **physical limitations** — neck pain, back pain, arthritis in the paws. Every snapping motion may cause pain, so the dog can't effectively follow through.
- The dog may also have been so well-trained that it expects the *owner* to handle corrections (it sees you as the leader — so lead!).
- Six years ago, the same dog corrected a previous puppy effectively — confirming the physical decline theory.

**Advice:**
1. **You finish what the older dog starts.** When the older dog barks at the puppy — you complete the correction. Step in, be firm with the puppy.
2. **Apply the six hierarchy rules strictly** to the puppy immediately. Reduce the puppy's freedom and boldness. This also makes life easier for the older dog.
3. Specific actions: puppy follows you to the kitchen? "Out of my space, big grizzly bear — out of the kitchen!" Puppy jumps up? Knee up, "No" (English), tap on the head if needed.
4. **Create a situation** where the older dog can deliver a proper correction: drop a bag of food on the floor. The older dog eats. The puppy inevitably approaches → the older dog corrects more seriously when food is at stake.
5. The puppy is 10 weeks old and has been home for 2 weeks — between 8–12 weeks they're establishing their place in the pack. Between 12–16 weeks "the gates open" — biting gets worse, they start biting back when corrected. The hierarchy rules need to be firmly in place before then.

## 7. Q&A: Puppy Won't Eat Kibble (Ollie's Case)

**Jaap** (participant) explains: Ollie, their female Golden Retriever (~11 weeks old, 8.5 kg — about a kilo overweight), came from a breeder who fed a diet heavy in raw meat alongside kibble. They've reduced the meat but still feed it twice daily. Ollie barely eats her kibble — sometimes she'll eat a training treat or two inside, but often turns her nose up. Outside, she's completely uninterested in food — only wants to run, pull, sniff, and eat leaves. Training is nearly impossible outdoors.

**Chantal's answer: Eliminate the meat completely.**

**Her reasoning:**
- The dog is so full and so accustomed to "steak and lobster" that kibble has zero value. Why would she work for kibble when she's getting gourmet meals for free?
- The breeder's philosophy of varied food (building digestive resilience) has merit — but the practical consequence is a puppy with no food drive and no bond with its owners through food.
- Ollie is becoming more connected to the *environment* (smells, other dogs, leaves) than to her *owners*. The owners are becoming secondary. At her age, that's unacceptable.

**Chantal's personal experience (21 years ago):**
- Her own Australian Shepherd from the same type of breeder (raw meat + kibble) refused to eat.
- The breeder said "Aussies are picky eaters, use cheese cubes." Chantal hung up and decided to go her own way.
- She offered kibble. The dog chose not to eat. **For 8 days.**
- He started spitting bile. He lost weight (but had been overweight — a dominant pup who hogged all the mother's teats and pushed siblings away).
- "He was happy, playful, drinking water, healthy. He *chose* not to eat. Not me. I *offered* it."
- "You're not a bad owner. The dog is just spoiled — it only wants filet mignon, shrimp, lobster, and oysters."
- Eventually, the dog started eating kibble and developed a healthy food drive.

**The gerbil analogy:**
Chantal currently has two gerbils (Oreo and Brownie). Internet advice says to always keep food available or they'll get stressed. She disagreed: "I also had stress during COVID, but I got smarter about my courses — I had to adapt." She withheld food for a day, then called their names — they came running. Now they come when called, do tricks, respond to "round" (making a circle). "It's not rocket science — you just need to find the balance." They won't die. They'll get smarter and more bonded.

**Jaap's response:** Acknowledges it's a big step but will do it. Notes that Chantal's tip from the previous week about crate sleeping worked immediately — Ollie slept in the crate right away after the change.

## 9. Case Studies from the Lesson

### The Sheep-Chasing Dog (Why Farm Animal Workshops Matter)

A client's dog — her second dog trained with Chantal, completing the obedience course properly — was left with family during their skiing holiday. Despite clear instructions to keep the dog leashed, the family let it run free near sheep. Day one: the dog ran back and forth along a ditch separating it from the sheep. Day two: it jumped the ditch, chased two sheep into the water, and severely injured one.

At the behavioral consultation, Chantal walked the dog past the local petting zoo. The dog went berserk — not just at goats, but at birds, ducks (currently in mating season, with drakes recklessly chasing hens), and anything on the ground. After an hour of work, the barking stopped, replaced by subdued whining, and the dog started turning its back on the animals — a sign of self-regulation.

The myth that "once a dog has tasted blood, it can never be untrained" is **absolutely false** — but rehabilitation requires significant work.

### Video Analysis: Hierarchy Between Two Dogs (Bulldog Example)

A client sent a video during COVID lockdown asking: "My younger dog keeps terrorizing the older dog. What should I do?"

Chantal used the video to demonstrate hierarchy rules in action:

The white dog (older, dominant) vs. the brown dog (younger, subordinate). In the video, four of the six hierarchy rules are visible in minutes:
- **Higher positioning:** The dominant dog asserts physical height
- **Who approaches whom:** The dominant dog controls proximity
- **Who makes way:** The subordinate moves aside
- **Who controls play:** The dominant dog doesn't want the younger one playing with toys freely — its body language says "Put the toys away — if he gets to play with everything, I can't rest because my leadership is undermined"

The younger dog repeatedly rolls onto its back (submitted completely) — not for fun, but because it loses every confrontation. Even when it tries to nip the other dog's paw, it rolls over. A "big loser" in rank — but this is natural hierarchy at work.

**Chantal's takeaway:** "People say hierarchy rules are outdated? Dogs enforce them 24/7. Next week, you'll learn to set boundaries yourselves — but your dogs are already doing it."

### Labrador vs. Dachshund: On-Leash Communication Failure

During a walk that afternoon, Chantal observed a Labrador approaching a Dachshund from higher ground (already physically threatening). The Labrador was "cheerfully oblivious" — no blinking, no calming signals, walking straight toward the Dachshund with its characteristic Labrador "the whole world is a party" energy.

The Dachshund froze, barked warnings. The Labrador ignored every signal and walked right up — then the Dachshund snapped and chased it. The Labrador jumped away, startled, sniffed around disinterestedly for a moment... then came back for another look. "That's having a board in front of your head" (Dutch expression for cluelessness).

**Chantal's perspective:** The Labrador got a free lesson. Its owner would have to pay good money for that correction. Labs, Golden Retrievers, Boxers, American Staffordshires — big, hairy, clumsy breeds — need early confrontations with dogs that dare to correct them. Chantal's own older dog (Jade) gave excellent corrections to Labradors. Her younger dog didn't — and the difference in behavior was noticeable throughout the younger dog's life.

The small-breed problem: Cavaliers, Cockapoos, Poodles, Toy Poodles, Pomeranians, Jack Russells, Dachshunds — these dogs are small their entire lives. They're constantly jumped on by larger dogs. Eventually, every four-legged thing becomes "the enemy." This is how reactive "yappy small dogs" are created — not because they're inherently badly behaved, but because they've been overwhelmed repeatedly. It's discrimination born from negative experience, just like human prejudice.

**Chantal draws the parallel explicitly:** discrimination in dogs and humans comes from the same place — limited experience leading to generalized fear. Her extended analogy about Turkish and Moroccan people being perceived differently at home versus as vacation hosts, and about people judging others by accent or social class, reinforces the point: we project our limited experience onto entire groups. Dogs do the same. The cure is the same: more positive, controlled exposure.
