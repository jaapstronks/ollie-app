# Corrections & Boundaries

How to set boundaries and correct unwanted behavior effectively. The key is understanding when and how to correct, and being consistent.

---

## The Correction Word

- Use a consistent word like "no" as your correction/boundary word.
- **Important:** if your dog's name sounds like "no" (Dino, Bono, Nomi, Noah, Bruno, Bo, Kato), pick a different neutral correction word.
- Don't use common words you say casually in conversation — the dog will think they're being corrected when they're not.
- The word itself becomes a signal because of the association. Like how the clicker means "reward is coming," the correction word means "stop that."

---

## Direct vs Indirect Correction

Understanding the difference is crucial:

### Direct Correction (Person-Linked)

A direct correction is **tied to you personally**. The dog learns: "I shouldn't do this _when my owner is present_."

**Only use direct correction for behaviors that can only happen when you're there:**

- Biting your hands/feet
- Jumping up against you
- Walking in front of your feet
- Pushing through a doorway
- Demand-barking directed at you
- Being disobedient in your presence

**The formula:** Correction word + consequence → wait for calm response → 20 seconds of rest

- Keep applying consequences until the dog calms down (no longer pulling, jumping, or pushing).
- If you stop before the dog settles, you're just giving attention, not actually correcting.
- The 20 seconds of rest afterward lets the lesson land.
- **Do not over-correct.** If the dog already calms down quickly, you stop immediately.

**Examples of appropriate consequences:**

- Turning away / withdrawing attention
- A firm stance / direct eye contact
- A knee up when the dog jumps against you (large dog hits knee, small dog hits foot)
- Pushing the door closed when the dog tries to go through first
- Pulling the food bowl up when the dog dives in
- **Time-out:** briefly removing the dog to another room for 20 seconds

### Indirect Correction (Location-Linked)

An indirect correction teaches the dog: "I should never do this _at this location/with this object_." The dog does NOT know the consequence came from you.

**Formula: Startle → Redirect → Comfort**

1. Something startles the dog near the problem location/object (without the dog seeing you cause it)
2. Pull the dog away / redirect attention
3. Comfort/soothe the dog

The startle + comfort sequence creates a negative association with that location/object.

**Rules for indirect correction:**

- The dog must NOT see you cause the startle
- The dog must NOT hear you say anything
- The dog must NOT see you staring at them
- If any of these happen, the correction becomes person-linked instead

**Use indirect correction for:**

- Eating off the street/ground
- Counter-surfing or stealing food from tables
- Digging holes in the garden
- Chewing cables/furniture when you're not nearby
- Going into the dishwasher, trash can, etc.

**Setting traps when you're not around:**

If the dog does something only when you're away, set up a self-triggering consequence:

- Put an object on top of the trash can so it falls when the dog pulls at it
- Stack something that will clatter on the fridge handle (for a dog that opens the fridge)

The key is that the location/object itself becomes "unpleasant" in the dog's mind. You may need to repeat multiple times depending on how rewarding the behavior is.

## Hand-Biting / Mouthing

Puppy hand-biting has two very different causes — handle them differently:

### Overstimulation (NOT a correction situation)

If the puppy is overtired/overstimulated and bites your hands:

- **Do not correct.** The dog is communicating "I can't cope anymore."
- Put the dog to rest more frequently (crate)
- Find the spots the dog actually enjoys being touched
- Stop petting roughly over the head

### Actual boundary-testing (correction appropriate)

If the dog is deliberately biting to get a reaction or test limits:

- Use the direct correction formula
- If the dog bites your fingers while taking food: close your fist around the treat and wait for gentle behavior
- Wait for calm licking instead of snapping
- Release the treat — the dog learns to be gentle

This teaches soft mouth: "You can have the food, but only if you're gentle."

---

## Teaching Gentle Treat-Taking

If the dog chomps your whole hand when taking treats:

- Close your fist around the treat
- Wait
- The dog will try biting, licking, pawing
- Only open your hand when the dog backs off or licks gently
- The message: "Take the treat, not the fist."

The dog learns to use lips and tongue carefully instead of snapping.

---

## Object Guarding & Taking Things from the Dog's Mouth

- **Never grab things from the dog's mouth.** This teaches resource guarding, clamping down, or swallowing dangerous items faster.
- **For harmless items** (plastic bottle, leaf, stick, cardboard): let the dog carry it. Let them be proud of their find. After carrying it many times, the novelty wears off and they drop it voluntarily.
- **For dangerous items** (sharp object, toxic item): scatter a handful of kibble on the ground near the dog. The sound and sight of falling kibble is irresistible — the dog drops the dangerous item and goes for the food. Pick up the dangerous item while the dog eats.

### Training "Drop It" / Trade

- "Drop it" should always mean "you get a better deal" — never mean "I pry open your mouth"
- Hold a treat against the dog's nose while they hold a toy. They release the toy → they get a good trade.
- Consistently trade with high-value treats so the dog learns: bringing things to you = jackpot
- A well-trained trade means the dog proudly brings you found items instead of hiding with them

## Jumping Up

If the dog jumps on you uninvited:

- Bring your knee up as a block (large dog hits knee, small dog gets your foot raised)
- Pair with your correction word
- Dogs naturally approach politely from a low position — jumping is rude behavior that needs correcting

---

## Walking Through Doors

At doorways (front door, crate door, room entrances):

- Open the door. If the dog tries to rush through — close it immediately, pair with correction word.
- Open again. Dog tries again → close. Repeat.
- The goal is that the dog waits for you naturally, not that they're commanded into position.
- This is practical safety training — you don't want a dog bolting out the front door into traffic.

---

## Food Bowl Manners

Teaching the dog to wait calmly before eating:

1. Put a few kibbles in the bowl, lower it toward the ground
2. If the dog dives for it → correction word → pull the bowl up
3. Wait 20 seconds
4. Lower again — the dog will wait slightly longer this time
5. Repeat until the dog looks at you instead of diving
6. Remove your hand from the bowl, step back
7. If the dog lunges → correction word → bowl goes up again
8. Eventually the dog waits calmly → give a release word ("enjoy," "free," etc.)

For dogs that bark/get hyper before mealtime:

- Put the dog on leash
- Start preparing food — if the dog barks → time-out (20 seconds)
- Bring back, try again — barks again → time-out again
- Train this during evening meals first (less time pressure than mornings)

---

## Consistency Is Everything

- If you correct the dog for something, you must be prepared to correct it **every single time**.
- If you can't supervise, prevent the behavior instead (leash, crate, close doors).
- Variable correction (sometimes yes, sometimes no) teaches the dog to keep trying because sometimes they get away with it.
- Everyone in the household must enforce the same rules.
