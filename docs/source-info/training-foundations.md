# Training Foundations

Core methodology for puppy training: clicker conditioning, operant vs classical conditioning, food motivation, and general principles.

---

## The Clicker: Setup & Rules

- **Condition the clicker first** before using it for any exercise. Pair click → treat, click → treat repeatedly until the association is solid.
- **Muffle the clicker initially** — put it under a cushion, wrap it in a scarf or sweater. Gradually expose the dog to the full volume so the sound isn't overwhelming.
- **Test the clicker**: let the dog wander to another room, then click. If the dog comes running back expecting a treat, the clicker is properly conditioned. This is a one-time test only — don't use the clicker as a recall tool.
- **Never click without treating.** Every single click must be followed by a reward. Clicking without a treat destroys the association you've built.
- **Clicker or marker word — pick one, not both.** You can use a clicker or a training word like "yes." Don't mix them.
- **The clicker is not permanent.** You need it to train behaviors in various environments. Once a behavior is solid across many contexts, you can phase the clicker out. But variable reward must continue for life.

## Two Training Styles

### Operant Conditioning (Free Shaping) — Passive Training

The dog figures things out on its own. You do nothing except observe and click for desired behavior.

- **You are passive.** Hands behind your back. No luring, no pushing, no verbal cues. You simply wait.
- **Train in a low-stimulus environment.** Your living room, with no cats, children, or other dogs present. The dog needs to be able to focus one-on-one with you.
- **What counts as "low-stimulus" depends on your dog.** Some dogs are so food-focused that they tune out distractions completely.
- **Add the verbal command last.** Only when the dog reliably performs the behavior in multiple different environments (living room, kitchen, garden, front door) do you attach a word to it.
- **Best for:** the "attention" (look at me) exercise, teaching calm behaviors.

### Classical Conditioning (Luring) — Active Training

You actively guide the dog with a food lure in your hand.

- **You are active.** You use a treat in your hand to steer the dog into position.
- **Can be used in stimulating environments** — this is why it's used on the training field with other dogs around, where waiting passively would take forever.
- **The hand signal is introduced immediately.** The luring motion becomes the non-verbal command from the start.
- **No verbal commands** during training. Work only with hand signals. Words cause humans to repeat, escalate, and pressure ("sit, sit, SIT!"). Hand signals can't be made "louder" or more forceful.
- **Best for:** sit, down, heel/follow exercises.

### Combining Both Styles

You can mix approaches. For example, if luring doesn't work well for "down" with certain breeds (dachshunds, French bulldogs), switch to shaping it passively instead.

## Food & Motivation

### Use regular kibble as training rewards

- **No fancy treats for regular training.** No dried chicken, salmon bites, or commercial "training treats." Use the dog's normal daily kibble as reward.
- **A different brand of kibble is okay** for higher-value situations (e.g., a new environment). Pick a different flavor — lamb & rice kibble smells strong and dogs love it. Once the dog settles into the environment, go back to regular kibble.
- **Reserve the highest-value rewards** (actual special treats) exclusively for the recall command ("come here"). Nothing else.

### Create hunger and motivation

- **Feed from your fingers, not a bowl.** The dog earns its daily food through training.
- **If you also feed raw/fresh meat:** schedule it as far from training time as possible. Training in the evening? Give meat in the morning.
- **No food 8 hours before class.** If class is at 7:30 PM, the last meal is at noon.
- **If the dog is a picky eater:** skip an entire day of food if needed. This is not cruel — in nature, dogs don't eat every day. A dog that won't work for food is too "wealthy" to bother.
- **The packaging overestimates portion sizes.** Manufacturers want you to buy more. Cut at least a third off the recommended amount.
- **The "reset" matters more than immediate progress.** If you can't train because the dog won't eat, don't panic. A motivated dog progresses fast. An unmotivated dog will never progress.
- **Watch the ribs.** If they become too visible, increase food again. But err on the leaner side during the motivation reset.

### If your dog refuses kibble (raised on raw/fresh)

- **Cut out the fresh meat.** The dog won't starve — they'll adjust.
- A dog that's constantly full has no motivation to work with you.
- It's normal for a dog to skip meals for several days during the transition. As long as they're drinking water, acting happy, and are otherwise healthy — they're choosing not to eat.
- Once hunger returns, they'll start valuing kibble — and valuing _you_ as the source of food.

## Why to Avoid Verbal Commands (Initially)

- **Humans misuse words.** We repeat, escalate, add pressure. "Sit. Sit. SIT!" teaches the dog that the command is actually "sit-sit-SIT" with increasing anger.
- **Words get paired with the wrong behavior.** Saying "down" while pushing the dog teaches "down" = being pushed. Saying "drop it" while prying something from the dog's mouth teaches "drop it" = someone's about to steal my stuff → clamp harder or swallow.
- **Saying "quiet" while the dog barks** literally labels the barking. You're telling the dog "bark" = "quiet."
- **The fork/spoon analogy:** If someone holds up a spoon while saying "fork," you associate the word with the wrong object. Dogs experience the same confusion when we pair words with the wrong actions.
- **Stick to hand signals.** They can't be made "angry," can't be repeated louder, and if the dog isn't looking, no harm is done.

## Environment & Generalization

### The step-back principle

- Every exercise has a **starting step and an end goal.** You can move between them at any time depending on the environment.
- If a behavior works perfectly at home but falls apart at the training field → **go back to step 1.** Don't repeat the verbal cue louder. Help the dog by lowering the difficulty.
- A dog that has done "down" perfectly for two years may refuse to lie down in snow because it feels strange. Start from step 1 again.
- **Never forget the first steps of any exercise.** They're your fallback for any new situation.

### Training location progression

1. Master exercises indoors (living room)
2. In front of the house / doorstep
3. In town / quiet streets
4. At the park
5. At the beach / busy environments

Only move to a new environment when the previous one is solid.

### Exposure and desensitization

- **Puppies freeze on walks** because the environment is overwhelming. If you need them to potty, carry them to the potty zone, set them down, and let them take their time.
- **Repetition lowers arousal.** The more you visit the same place, the less exciting it becomes.
- **Go outside frequently in short sessions.** Two to three times per day, just step outside the front door, do a few reps of an exercise, and go back inside.
- **Don't train during the whole walk.** Dedicate training to short, focused doorstep sessions. Walks are for experiencing the world.
- **If energy is too high, the dog can't learn.** Wait for the energy to come down before asking for anything.

### Raising reward value for harder environments

- When the environment gets harder, you can raise the reward value — but **don't jump to special treats.** Use a different brand/flavor of kibble first.
- Special treats (chicken, salmon) are reserved **only for the recall command.**

## General Principles

- **If a behavior is no longer rewarded, it disappears.** This is a fundamental rule.
- **If a behavior is variably rewarded, it persists forever.** The "slot machine effect" — not knowing when the next reward comes keeps the dog eager and willing.
- **Variable punishment also makes behavior persist.** If you sometimes correct barking and sometimes ignore it, the ignoring functions as approval. The behavior never extinguishes.
- **Only move to the next step when the current step is effortless.** No exceptions. Rushing creates confusion.
- **You're training the human 80%, the dog 20%.** The biggest source of error is handler behavior, not the dog's ability.
- **Film yourself training.** Watching yourself on video reveals mistakes you genuinely don't notice in the moment.
- **Be consistent.** If you correct the dog for something, you must be prepared to correct it every single time. If you can't supervise, prevent the behavior instead.
- **All food can be training food.** Reduce or eliminate "free" meals from the bowl and use kibble throughout the day for training.

## Equipment

- **Leash:** 1.80–2 meters, fixed length.
- **No retractable leashes.** They teach pulling, they can drop and scare the puppy, and they offer zero control.
- **Collar:** Regular flat collar. No slip chains.
- **Harness:** Okay to use, but **always have a regular collar on as well.** Some exercises require the collar — a harness twists sideways and the dog can escape.
- **Clicker** in left hand, **treats** in right hand. Standard for consistent instructions.

## Health Note: Giardia

- Very common in puppies — often contracted from the breeder/nest.
- **Key symptom:** alternating between normal and loose stool. Not just persistent diarrhea, but inconsistent firmness.
- Feed only regular food (no chews, no extras) and monitor. If stool inconsistency continues on a stable diet, have the vet check for giardia.
- **Avoid raw hide chews and pig ears** for young puppies — they're very salty, cause excessive drinking, and mess up housetraining.
- Keep food consistent so you can properly read the dog's digestion and potty timing.
