# Practical Living with Your Dog

Day-to-day tips for crate training, alone time, leash management, handling equipment, children, and common problem behaviors.

---

## The Crate

The crate is not a punishment — it's a tool for rest and independence.

### Why crates matter

- Dogs who are given too much free roaming and follow you everywhere (room to room, up and down stairs) are learning the opposite of independence.
- If your dog screams in the crate, that means you're using it too little, not that it doesn't work.
- Put the dog in more often — it gets calmer with use, not less positive.
- The crate teaches the dog that being separate from you is normal and safe.

### Common uses

- Rest time when the puppy is overstimulated
- Meal times (prevents food guarding with other pets/children)
- When you can't supervise (prevents unwanted behavior)
- Travel safety
- Vet visits / grooming appointments

## Teaching Alone Time (Passive Stay)

This exercise teaches the dog to be independent and calm when separated from you — the foundation for being left alone.

### How to train it

1. Tie the dog to something sturdy in the living room (table leg, radiator) with a **short** leash — just enough to stand, sit, and lie down
2. Don't give any commands (no "sit," no "stay," no "lie down")
3. Walk 3–5 meters away, **with your back to the dog**
4. Watch via a mirror, phone camera, or have someone watch
5. The moment the dog sits or lies down → **click** → turn around → walk back → reward
6. Toss a treat behind the dog so it can sniff around briefly
7. Repeat **3 times per session**, same location
8. Do 3–4 sessions per day with breaks between them

### Key points

- Keep the leash short — less room to sniff means the dog runs out of things to do faster and settles sooner
- Don't pace back and forth (creates nervousness) — just stand still
- Don't say any words during the exercise
- Only **after** the dog reliably understands the pattern do you add a "stay" cue
- First master it at one location, then introduce a second location

### Why this matters

- At a friend's: tie the dog to a tree in the shade, the dog relaxes while activity happens nearby
- Quick errand: tie the dog to a post outside a shop, visible through the glass, dog lies down calmly
- General independence: the dog learns that you always come back, reducing separation anxiety

## Putting On a Harness or Collar

If the dog bites/fights when you try to put on the harness:

- Don't do it in a cramped hallway while rushing to go outside
- Sit on the couch in a calm moment, with treats
- For small dogs: lift them onto the couch to work at your level
- Scatter kibble between your feet on the floor — while the dog eats, calmly put on the harness/collar
- The dog is focused on eating and barely notices your hands
- Same technique for clipping on a leash during recall: scatter treats on the ground, clip the leash while the dog is distracted eating

If you don't get this right, you risk creating a dog that won't let you leash them at all.

## Leash Biting

If your puppy bites the leash during walks:

- The dog is **bored and looking for action.** The solution isn't to pry the leash out — it's to give the dog something better to do.
- **Use training:** Click and treat for eye contact, for walking beside you, for any movement that looks like calm following.
- The dog switches from "leash game" to "treat game."

## Dog Barking at the Dinner Table

If the dog barks while the family eats:

- The dog is overstimulated from watching everyone move around and suddenly everything stops
- Put the dog on leash, foot on leash, wrap it around your thigh — hands free, dog can stand/sit/lie but nothing else
- Give NO commands — just restrict space and wait for the dog to settle
- If it barks (directed at you): time-out in the hallway for 20 seconds, then bring back
- With repetition, the dog learns: settle = stay with the family, bark = isolation

## Eating Off the Street

For dogs that eat everything off the ground:

- Use indirect correction — set up training scenarios
- Bring your own "bait" items, drop them on the ground in a training location
- When the dog goes for it: startle (throw something they don't see you throw), redirect, comfort
- Do this in a location that's NOT your regular walking route
- **Simultaneously:** train excellent "follow" so the dog is focused on earning treats from you rather than scanning the ground

---

## Counter-Surfing

For dogs that steal food from counters/tables:

- Use indirect correction
- Set up a scenario: put something tempting on the table edge, attached to something that will clatter when pulled down
- The dog knocks it over → startling noise → negative association with the surface
- The location itself becomes "not worth it" in the dog's mind
- May need to repeat multiple times depending on how rewarding the behavior was

## Couch Rules

A dog can be on the couch — if there are no behavioral issues and the dog handles being alone:

- The dog comes up when _you_ invite them and gets off when _you_ say so
- This is about boundaries, not punishment
- Keep it simple: invitation up, clear signal to get off

## Overstimulated Puppy Outside (Can't Focus)

For young puppies (4–5 months) who can't focus during outdoor training:

- Don't retreat back inside — stay outside but keep sessions very short (5 minutes)
- Use the car as a rest station: get out → train briefly → back in the car for 5 minutes → out again
- This "micro-exposure" approach (in and out 4 times in 20 minutes) neutralizes the excitement much faster than one long session
- Reduce food rations from the bowl — all food is earned through training
- Don't give away leftover kibble for free — keep it for the next session

## Children and Puppies

### Safety rules

- Children must stay upright — sitting/lying on the ground invites jumping and biting
- Children should not run through the room (triggers chasing/biting)
- Children should not approach the dog — the dog should come to them on invitation
- Young toddlers often instinctively correct dogs well (swatting away) — don't always suppress that natural reaction, as the dog learns respect from it

### Older children (8–10+)

- Can be empowered to correct the dog themselves
- If the dog bites uninvited, a firm push away is appropriate self-defense
- Teaching children to set boundaries helps both the child and the dog

## Where and How to Touch Your Dog

Most people pet dogs wrong. What dogs typically do NOT enjoy:

- Being petted over the top of the head (they'll snap at your hands)
- Being rubbed on the belly when lying on their back (they're actually showing submission, not asking for belly rubs)

**Spots dogs actually enjoy:**

- Under the collar
- On the breastbone
- Between the front legs (slightly lower than the chest)
- At the base of the tail (where the tail begins, above the butt)

**Belly rubs — important nuance:**

- A dog rolling onto their back is a **submission signal**, not necessarily an invitation
- A dog lying on their **side** relaxed, kicking along as you scratch → they're genuinely enjoying it
- That's different from a full belly-up submission when you approach
- If the dog flips onto their back when you approach: **wait.** Don't pet yet. Let them relax first.

## Reducing Free Roaming

- **Reduce the dog's free roaming space** rather than enlarging your own fenced-off area
- The dog's space should be managed, not unlimited
- This prevents problems before they start and makes supervision practical

---

## Hide and Seek (For Off-Leash Dogs)

A fun way to teach your dog to keep track of you:

- If you dare to let the dog off-leash, play hide and seek in nature
- Don't pick the first tree next to the path — really hide
- Consider wind direction so the dog can't find you by smell too quickly
- Let the dog feel a moment of concern when they can't find you — this teaches them to keep an eye on you in the future
- Even on the beach: lie flat on your stomach, head down. The dog is looking for a standing silhouette, so this already counts as "hidden."

---

## Making Your Movement Unpredictable

On off-leash walks:

- Deliberately walk in unexpected directions. Don't stick to the path.
- When the dog is far ahead, suddenly turn and walk in the opposite direction
- The dog will be surprised, look around, and run after you
- This teaches the dog to pay attention to where YOU are, not just explore independently
- Be adventurous — go through nature, change direction often
