# Basic Exercises

Step-by-step training guides for core commands: Attention, Sit, Down, and Follow/Heel.

---

## Exercise 1: Attention ("Look at me")

This is trained **purely with operant conditioning** (passive/shaping). The goal is to get the dog to make eye contact with you reliably.

### Steps

1. **Get on the dog's level.** Sit on the floor. For very small dogs, put the dog on the couch and sit on the floor so you're at eye level.
2. **Treats in right hand, clicker in left hand. Both hands behind your back.**
3. **Wait.** Say nothing. Don't make sounds. Don't move your hands. Don't lure.
4. **The instant the dog makes eye contact — click and treat.** Even a split-second glance at your face counts.
5. **Throw the treat away** so the dog has to move to get it, then naturally resets to standing position. This prevents the dog from only learning to give attention while sitting or lying down.
6. **Repeat.** The dog will look at your hands, look around, sniff the floor — and eventually glance at your face again. Click that moment.
7. **Gradually increase duration.** Once the dog starts reliably looking at you, wait for 1 second of eye contact before clicking. Then 2 seconds. Then 3.
8. **Change your position.** First on the floor → then on a chair → then standing. At each stage, the dog needs to find your face at a new height.
9. **Arms out to the sides.** The dog must choose: look at the hands (where treats come from) or look at your face. Reward only face/eye contact.
10. **Add the verbal cue ("look") only after** the dog reliably offers eye contact in multiple environments (living room, kitchen, garden, front door).
11. **Timing of the verbal cue:** say "look" at the moment the dog is _about to_ make eye contact — during the action toward your face, not while looking elsewhere.
12. **Test it:** when the dog is sniffing the ground and not looking at you, say the cue. If the dog looks up at you → it's trained. If not → go back to the previous step.

### Tips

- If the dog keeps going behind you, sit with your back against a wall or couch.
- A small sound to get the dog's general attention is okay, but the click and treat only come when actual eye contact happens.
- Don't worry about sit vs stand vs down during this exercise — the position doesn't matter, only the eye contact.

## Exercise 2: Sit

Trained with **classical conditioning** (luring). No verbal commands — hand signal only.

### Steps

1. **Treat on the dog's nose.** Slowly move it up and slightly back, over the head toward the ears (not toward the tail, or the dog walks backward). Click the moment the dog starts shifting weight backward into a sit. Treat.
2. **Hand slightly above the nose** (~10–15 cm). No longer touching the nose. The dog follows the visual of the hand going up, sits. Click and treat.
3. **Hand starts at your side, sweeps up to a 90° angle** (elbow to armpit). This becomes the final hand signal. The hand no longer goes to the dog's nose — it simply rises beside your face.

### Tips

- The final hand signal (hand up beside your face, palm forward) is visible even in a winter coat, and from a distance.
- Children naturally throw their hands up when excited — many dogs will sit for that gesture, which is a bonus.
- Always right hand for treats, left hand for leash and clicker.
- **Only move to the next step when the previous step is effortless.** No ambiguity, no hesitation.

## Exercise 3: Down

Trained with **classical conditioning** (luring), starting from the sit. More complex than sit.

### Step 1: Lure between the front paws

- Dog sits. Bring the treat to the nose, then slowly straight down toward the ground, ending **between the front paws.**
- **Do not let the dog push your hand away.** Maintain gentle pressure/contact. The dog will try to lick and dig at your fingers — hold steady.
- **Keep the line straight down from nose to floor.** If your hand drifts forward (away from the dog), the dog will stand up and walk to follow it. You lose the sit-to-down combination.
- Click and treat for any downward movement: nose dipping, an elbow bending, weight shifting forward.

### Step 2: Tether the dog

- Attach the leash to something sturdy, or **put your foot on the leash** so the dog can't reach your hand.
- Lure toward the ground but keep the hand **just out of reach** (~5 cm gap). The dog wants to get to your fingers, can't, and eventually slides into a down. Click and treat.
- This teaches the dog that it's the _lying down_ that earns the reward — not the nose-pushing and licking.

### Step 3: Add distance (≈1 meter)

- Increase the gap between your hand and the dog. The dog learns: "I need to lie down over here, and the treat comes to me."
- This removes the "superstitious" behaviors (push, lick, then down) and isolates the down itself.
- **Only progress to this step when step 2 is smooth.**

### Step 4: Build height gradually

- **Ankle height:** Small sweep down near the ground. Dog follows.
- **Knee height:** Hand is higher — the dog can no longer follow it down visually. Must think: "that gesture means go down." Click for any intention movement toward the ground (looking down, bending an elbow). Be patient.
- **Hip height:** Dog must fully self-initiate the down from a standing visual cue.
- **Final: navel/waist height with a sideways sweep.** This is the target hand signal. The hand sweeps to the side — **not pulled backward** behind your body, but **sideways.**

### Building duration in the down

- After the dog lies down, move your hand **down to your ankle quickly, then back up.** The dog thinks the hand is still near the ground and stays put.
- Return route = same as exit route. Sneak the hand down behind your calves → up behind your knees → behind your thighs → behind your back. The dog never sees the hand rise in front of them.
- Gradually increase time: 5 seconds → 8 → 10 → longer.
- **To keep the dog in the down** (e.g., at a restaurant): wrap the end of the leash under your foot or around your thigh so the dog physically can't stand.

### Breed-specific notes

- **Dachshunds:** Their neck is longer than their legs — they can look at your hand at ground level while standing. Every increment toward down is tiny. Requires more patience.
- **French Bulldogs:** Limited neck flexibility. They may stick their butt up as they try to dive their head down. Requires more patience.
- **Dobermans and tall breeds:** Long stiff legs, short neck — going all the way down is physically uncomfortable for a large puppy. They'll eventually get there as it becomes familiar.
- **If luring doesn't work for down:** switch to operant/shaping. Click for any movement toward the ground — nose dipping, one elbow bending, looking down, weight shift. No pushing, no pulling.

## Exercise 4: Follow / Heel

The goal: dog walks beside you, looking up at your face, on a loose leash.

### Step 1: Hand on nose, walking backward

- Hold your hand so low that the dog does not need to jump. Even if that means very low for a small/young dog.
- **Stand still** when you reward. Click → stop → give treat → wait until the dog finishes eating → then start walking again.
- Keep the hand about **30 cm away from your knee** (not against your leg), so the dog walks beside you with space.
- Keep your hand loaded with treats — don't keep reaching into your pocket between reps.
- If the dog jumps, your hand was too high.
- **Short sessions:** 5 minutes in the living room, then stop. Multiple short sessions throughout the day.

### Step 2: Hand stretched out to the side, up high

- From step 1, pull your hand straight out to the side and up — **level with your face**, not hip height.
- Common mistake: hand too low. It must be high enough that the dog looks up toward your face.
- Don't angle the hand like a "sit" signal — stretch it fully sideways.
- After each click and treat in step 2, **restart from step 1**: walk 2–3 steps with hand low on the nose, then bring it up to step 2 again.
- You don't need to reward every step 1 — sometimes yes, sometimes no.

### Step 3: The rotation (switching sides)

- With your arm stretched out to the right, the dog is on your right. Now they need to end up on your left.
- **Rotate your left shoulder inward** so your body turns, but your hand stays in exactly the same place.
- The dog doesn't notice any position change — they're still under the same hand.
- Practice tip: put your hand against a wall. The hand can't move. Now rotate your body. That's the movement.

### Step 4: Hand moves across chest

- Move your right hand from your left shoulder across your chest to your right shoulder, then behind your back.
- This removes the hand from the dog's sight so it looks up at your face instead of following the hand.

### Step 5: Eye contact while walking

- Once the hand is behind your back, the dog should look up at your face.
- Click and reward for eye contact.
- You should be able to walk at least **10 straight meters** with the dog looking at your face and the hand behind your back before moving to step 6.

### Step 6: Forward start

- Instead of starting by walking backward (steps 1–3), start by stepping forward with your **left leg** (closest to the dog).
- Make a sweeping motion with the treat hand toward the dog's nose, then bring the hand to your shoulder and sweep it behind your back.

### Training Progression

- Always start each session from step 1, work through to step 5.
- Gradually reduce rewards at earlier steps (sometimes reward at step 1, sometimes skip to step 3, sometimes hold off until step 5).
- Once step 5 works for 10+ meters indoors → add step 6 indoors.
- Once step 6 is solid indoors → take the whole sequence outside.
- Outside: start at easy locations (in front of your door), then gradually move to busier environments.
- **Stop training indoors once it works there.** Only train outside. The goal is outdoor obedience.

## The Release Word ("Free")

Train a release word to signal "you're off duty":

- Throw a few leaves in the air and say "free" as the dog chases them.
- Or toss 3 treats in the grass and say "search, search, free."
- During free time, keep saying "free, free, free" so the dog links the behavior to the word.

---

## Praise & Encouragement During Training

- **Keep exercises quiet and calm.** Don't narrate or cheerfully chat throughout. The dog learns: if there's constant talking, I'm doing it right. Silence = something's wrong.
- **Save verbal praise for genuinely exceptional moments.** Example: the dog walks past another dog that lunges at them, turns away, and focuses on you. _That_ deserves an enthusiastic party.
- **End sessions with verbal praise + a gentle pet.** Pet under the chin (not over the head). Then put equipment away. Session over.
